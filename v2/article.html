Goal 13 · Climate Action

Youth climate councils win a seat at 40 city budget tables

From Nairobi to Jakarta, under-25 delegates now sign off on municipal climate spending — a quiet shift that is redrawing who decides, and how fast.

By Staff Reporter
Published 1 September 2026 · 8 min read · Global

Three years ago, the idea that a nineteen-year-old would hold a formal vote on a city's climate budget would have sounded like a school exercise. Today it is written into municipal statute in forty cities across four continents, and the delegates are not there to observe.

The model is broadly the same everywhere it has been adopted. A council of between fifteen and forty young residents is selected by open application, sits for a two-year term, and is given a defined share of the municipal climate budget to allocate. What varies — and what determines whether the exercise means anything — is whether that allocation is binding.

Where it is binding, spending shifts

In the eleven cities where the council's decision cannot be overturned by the executive, spending has moved measurably towards adaptation and away from prestige infrastructure. Cooling centres, tree canopy and flood drainage consistently outrank signature projects when the people allocating the money expect to live with the results.

“We are not a consultation exercise. We sign the same document the treasurer signs.”

Where the council is advisory only, the pattern is different and less encouraging. Recommendations are accepted in principle and deferred in practice, and turnover among delegates is roughly triple that of the binding councils — a straightforward signal that people stop showing up when nothing follows from the meeting.

What the next two years decide

The open question is whether the model survives a change of administration. Six of the forty councils were established by mayors who have since left office, and in two of those cities the budget allocation has already been quietly reduced. The councils that were written into statute rather than established by executive order have so far proved harder to unwind.
